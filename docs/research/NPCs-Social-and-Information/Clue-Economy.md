# Clue Economy

_How sources spread information to players: redundancy, delivery, pacing and the cost of a miss. Neutral and descriptive._

## SRD 5.2.1 (CC-BY-4.0)

- No clue rule. The Study action calls to mind information about a clue, book or memory (SRD p.189, Glossary: Study). The Search action finds what is not obvious (SRD p.187). Clue delivery otherwise rests on GM discretion.

## Books

- DMG 2014 › Ch 3 › Adventure Types › Mysteries › Clues: clues point to the villain's identity by showing motive, means or opportunity; they may be verbal or physical; some mislead and need later correction; it is better to include too many clues than too few; redundant clues cover the case where the party overlooks some. The book weighs a solved-too-fast mystery (some DM disappointment, player accomplishment) against an unsolvable one (player frustration).
- DMG 2014 › Ch 3 › Mysteries › Suspects: a closed circle of suspects is a technique; a secret held by several suspects keeps the villain hidden.
- DMG 2014 › Ch 6 › Between Adventures › Foreshadowing (listed in DM-Craft-Map): hints toward future events, accepting that some go unused. Not re-read for this branch.
- TCoE › Ch 5 › Puzzles › Running Puzzles (DM-Craft-Map title entry): present, answer clarifying questions, do not give the solution. Not read here.
- CoS › Introduction › Adventure Structure (title entry): a prep card reading that fixes key item locations. Not read here.

## Web

- Three Clue Rule (https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/1118/roleplaying-games/three-clue-rule): for each conclusion the players must reach, provide at least three clues. The author reasons that players miss one, ignore another and misread a third. A single clue has several failure points: searching the right place, caring, passing a check and drawing the right inference. Clues map many-to-one to conclusions, and the author suggests listing conclusions and checking them off. Failed skill checks can hide a clue entirely. Red herrings are risky. Backup measures include villain agents moving against the PCs, the villain's next scheduled action creating a new scene, and as last resort the villain attacking. Fetched in part: the last 9,651 characters of the page text were not read.
- Making Clues (https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/46338/roleplaying-games/random-gm-tips-making-clues): start from the revelations the PCs need, then ask what in a place or person points to each; three per revelation is ideal; six clue types: static, flexible, proactive (delivered to the PCs, often by NPCs who approach them), reactive (found by research or canvassing), dynamic (improvised after an unanticipated sensible action) and unassigned (improvised from a revelation list alone, which the author doubts). The article does not mention dice.
- Running Mysteries: The Null Result (https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/49225/roleplaying-games/running-mysteries-the-null-result): a failed search for a clue that does not exist helps players drop false theories. The author warns against three fixes: changing the solution to fit the players' theories, improvising a clue so a search always succeeds, and revealing all clues automatically. Redundant clue design is the robust alternative. The article does not discuss NPC interviews.
- Node-based design, as summarized by a search result (leads in [[_grounding]], and https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/8202/roleplaying-games/advanced-node-based-design-part-5-the-two-prongs-of-mystery-design): the Three Clue Rule applies to leads between nodes; any three clues should lead to at least one conclusion, so the scenario keeps working while the PCs have somewhere to go next. A mystery has a concept solve (what happened) and a scenario solve (where to go next); a clue that explains but does not point to a node does not count toward keeping the scenario moving. Density varies: sparse early, more explicit deeper.
- Running Mysteries: Returning to the Scene of the Crime (https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/49595/roleplaying-games/running-mysteries-returning-to-the-scene-of-the-crime): clues persist; a proactive clue can send stuck PCs back and explain how to search differently; locations and people can change while the PCs are away.
- GUMSHOE, as summarized by a search result (Pelgrane Press pages returned 403; see [[_grounding]]): core clues are found automatically when a character applies a relevant investigative ability, with no roll; spends buy extra, non-essential clues; each key scene holds at least one core clue pointing to another scene; the system was built because failed rolls stalled mysteries.
- Lazy DM secrets and clues (https://slyflourish.com/eight_steps_2023.html): a secret is a short note of one or two sentences, not tied to where it will be found; the original advice was ten per session, and unrevealed ones carry forward if still relevant; discovery is improvised at the table. Mike Shea reportedly reveals about half of those prepared (search summary, https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/24034659-re-using-secrets-and-clues). Secret types named in a search summary: character, location, historical, NPC or villain, plot-revealing, and hooks (https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/21409111-types-of-secrets).
- Player-generated secrets (https://slyflourish.com/player-generated_secrets.html): the GM can adopt a player's guess about a detail as a secret.
- Roleplay and Intrigue (https://slyflourish.com/running_intrigue_adventures.html): information comes out through conversation, gradually, depending on the approach and the questions.
- Universal NPC Roleplaying Template (https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/37916/roleplaying-games/universal-npc-roleplaying-template): Key Info lists what the PCs should get from the NPC; listing it does not guarantee they find it, so the Three Clue Rule still applies.

## Where sources disagree

- Redundancy versus automatic delivery: the Three Clue Rule and DMG 2014 add redundancy and accept that rolls and attention may still miss; GUMSHOE removes the roll for core clues; the Null Result article warns against automatic revelation.
- How many clues at once: the Three Clue Rule asks for at least three per conclusion; the-stacks doctrine pair `one-clue` says at most one real clue per beat (see [[The-Stacks-NPCs-and-Information-Today]]). The two address different quantities (clues per conclusion across a scenario versus clues revealed per beat). No source gathered here reconciles them.
- Prep location: Making Clues assigns clues to places and people before play; Lazy DM prepares secrets detached from place and improvises discovery.
