# Failure, Consequences and Retries

_What sources say happens after a failed roll: fail-forward, proportionate consequences, locking an approach, repeated checks and retries. Neutral and descriptive._

## SRD 5.2.1 (CC-BY-4.0)

- Retry control is built into Influence: after a failed check the character waits 24 hours, or a GM-set duration, before urging the monster in the same way again. (SRD p.184, Influence)
- A save's result "is detailed in the effect that caused it" (SRD 5.2.1, CC-BY-4.0); the consequence of failure belongs to the effect. (SRD p.7, Saving Throws)
- A search conducted nowhere near the target cannot succeed; the consequence is wasted time, not a roll. (SRD p.12, Finding Hidden Objects)
- Consequence by terrain: failing the DC 10 Acrobatics check on a rough landing leaves the character Prone; failing to clear a low obstacle means the character hits it. (SRD p.179, p.185, Glossary: Climbing, Long Jump)
- A fall deals 1d6 bludgeoning per 10 feet (max 20d6) and leaves the creature Prone. A DC 15 Strength (Athletics) or Dexterity (Acrobatics) check when landing in water halves the damage. (SRD p.182, Glossary: Falling)

## Books

- DMG 2014 (Ch 8 > Using Ability Scores > Ability Checks > Multiple Ability Checks): two retry cases. Where failure costs only time, the character may retry; the DM can give automatic success for ten times the normal time taken, but no amount of retrying turns an impossible task into success. Where failure removes the approach (example: a lie to a guard that did not work), the same lie cannot be told again; the party must find another route or target, and the DM may make later checks harder.
- DMG 2014 (Ch 8 > Resolution and Consequences > Success at a Cost): the book's main fail-forward tool. A near miss becomes success plus a complication that changes the situation and gives the party new choices.
- DMG 2014 (Ch 8 > Resolution and Consequences > Degrees of Failure): graded failure keeps consequences proportionate; a bad miss escalates what happens next rather than ending the scene.
- DMG 2014 (Ch 8 > Social Interaction > 4. Repeat?): after the Charisma check, further attempts may be fruitless or may anger the target and shift its attitude toward Hostile. Another character may still repair it through clever roleplay and a successful Persuasion check.
- DMG 2014 (Ch 8 > Exploration > Tracking): a failed track check loses the trail. Re-finding it takes 10 minutes of careful search in a confined area or 1 hour outdoors. New checks are required after a rest, an obstacle, or a terrain or weather change.
- DMG 2014 (Ch 8 > Combat > Chases > Dashing): extra Dashes beyond 3 + Con modifier each require a DC 10 Constitution check or the creature gains a level of exhaustion; a scripted cost of repeated effort.
- DMG 2014 (Ch 8 > Chases > Ending a Chase): the quarry makes a Stealth check each round against the pursuers' highest passive Perception; if the quarry is in the lead pursuer's sight the check fails automatically. Each failed round continues the chase.
- DMG 2014 (Ch 9 > Combat Options > Morale): a failed morale save (DC 10 Wisdom) makes a monster flee or surrender. A fleeing monster can have side consequences for the party (alerting the dungeon, running off with treasure).
- DMG 2014 (Ch 8 > The Role of Dice > The Middle Path): the DM may turn an easy task into an impossible one through a bad plan or unfortunate circumstances, or impose disadvantage, which sets the floor on retry-ability.
- VRGtR (Horror Adventures > Running Horror Games > Horror Pacing > Just Enough Hope): players in a horror game should still have agency over consequences. The text advises against allies rescuing characters from problems they caused and against placing characters in impossible situations.
- TCoE (DM's Tools > Environmental Hazards): hazard effects mostly fail forward through damage or conditions on a failed save, with named DCs (DC 13 Constitution for a necrotic tempest, DC 15 Dexterity for flung objects), not through scene-ending failure.

## Web

The Alexandrian

- Failure for the Beginning GM (https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/46186/roleplaying-games/failure-for-the-beginning-gm): three techniques. (1) No retries: a failed check ends that method and players pick another approach. (2) Quick-and-dirty failing forward: a failure still advances but with a complication; if no complication comes to mind, grant success or fall back to no retries; optionally let the player choose to accept a cost. (3) A progress clock of four, six or eight segments filled on failed checks, which can be open, hidden or secret.
- Art of Rulings Part 8: Let it Ride (https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/38313/roleplaying-games/the-art-of-rulings-part-8-let-it-ride): one check per endeavor, whose result governs the whole attempt, because per-step checks compound the odds of failure (75 percent per check falls to about 42 percent after three and 23 percent after five). A point of uncertainty lets a near miss be saved by a further action. A ride ends on failure, a change of approach, a drastic change of circumstances or reaching a landmark. The article names let it ride the default roughly nine times in ten, with endurance checks as an exception.
- GM Don't List #2: Rolling to Failure (https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/38798/roleplaying-games/gm-dont-list-2-rolling-to-failure): two opposite problems. Chains of checks make planning pointless; repeated checks with no time pressure or cost (lockpicking) do too. Fixes: let it ride, a Take 20-style rule (if the character can eventually succeed, assume it), and grouping search checks into meaningful units. The author usually refuses requests for repeated checks, with an exception for deliberate pacing in tense moments.
- Random GM Tip: Dogpiling the Check (https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/49184/roleplaying-games/random-gm-tip-dogpiling-the-check): solutions for retries after a failed group member: no retries, a gradated group check (half of attempts must succeed), failing forward on the first attempt, and a progress clock. It also suggests attaching a benefit to each extra success and a risk to each extra failure so extra attempts are a real choice.
- Random GM Tip: Rolling for Riddles (https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/48452/roleplaying-games/random-gm-tip-rolling-for-riddles): make puzzles non-essential (alternate ways past) and roll for clues rather than for the solution, so a success gives partial information and a failure does not stall play.

Angry GM

- 5 Simple Rules for Dating My Teenaged Skill System (https://theangrygm.com/five-simple-rules-for-dating-my-teenaged-skill-system/): Rule 3, treat an action as one attempt and after a failure re-ask Rule 2. If the failure changed the situation (alerting an enemy), a new roll may be warranted; if nothing new is at risk, the retry succeeds automatically. Rule 3a allows multiple rolls when a visible countdown exists (time, money, hit points, materials), used sparingly. The article rejects codified penalty-for-failure retry rules as arbitrary.
- Adjudicate Actions Like a Boss (https://theangrygm.com/adjudicate-actions-like-a-boss/): after failure the character cannot simply retry, or must choose whether to. Consequences from the approach are tracked by the GM and never contradicted once established.
- Resolving Social Actions (https://theangrygm.com/resolving-social-actions/): a Patience track on each NPC ticks down with each social action. When it runs out the NPC stonewalls and no roll is made; the GM may also say openly when an encounter is clearly lost.

Sly Flourish

- Our Ability Check Toolbox (https://slyflourish.com/ability_check_toolbox.html): when the whole group fails a check that must yield vital information, the GM can give the information to the highest roller and fail forward with a complication (revealing position, missing a threat); the game keeps moving.
- Running Roleplay and Intrigue Adventures (https://slyflourish.com/running_intrigue_adventures.html): call checks when failure would be meaningful but not a dead end, so one bad roll does not close off whole paths.

## Where sources disagree

- Retries after failure. The Alexandrian offers no retries as technique 1 (https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/46186/roleplaying-games/failure-for-the-beginning-gm). Angry GM says that if nothing new is at risk the retry succeeds automatically and rejects codified retry penalties (https://theangrygm.com/five-simple-rules-for-dating-my-teenaged-skill-system/). Comments on the Alexandrian's Rolling to Failure record a view that removing Take 20 in 4E and 5E was a mistake (https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/38798/roleplaying-games/gm-dont-list-2-rolling-to-failure).
- Multiple rolls for one goal. The Alexandrian recommends one check per endeavor at roughly nine times in ten (https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/38313/roleplaying-games/the-art-of-rulings-part-8-let-it-ride). Angry GM allows multiple rolls only with a visible countdown (https://theangrygm.com/five-simple-rules-for-dating-my-teenaged-skill-system/). The difference is the trigger: the Alexandrian keys it to which option is more fun and to repeated same-skill checks that erase prior success; Angry GM keys it to visible rising threat or dwindling resources.
- Book versus web on retries: DMG 2014 allows a retry where failure costs only time and offers ten-times-time automatic success, while closing off a repeated approach where the failure removed it. The Alexandrian's no-retries default and Angry GM's retry-succeeds-if-nothing-new-is-at-risk both fall inside the same time-versus-approach split but name different defaults.
- Book versus web on pacing a retry loop: DMG 2014 converts a retry loop into automatic success by time; the Alexandrian offers let it ride and Take 20-style assumptions; Angry GM ties repeated rolls to a visible countdown.
- Book versus web on partial progress: DMG 2014 Degrees of Failure is a routine tool; Angry GM uses partial progress sparingly.
- Edition divergence: fail-forward tools (Success at a Cost, Degrees of Failure, ten-times-time success) exist only in DMG 2014 Ch 8; SRD 5.2.1 has no equivalent adjudication advice. SRD p.184 builds a retry lock into Influence (24 hours), a rule DMG 2014 social resolution replaces with a Repeat step that may anger the target.
- Edition divergence: exhaustion. SRD p.181 uses a cumulative scheme (-2 to D20 Tests and -5 feet of Speed per level, death at level 6). DMG 2014 Chases (Dashing) reflects the older six-level table (level 5 reduces Speed to 0) and does not match SRD 5.2.1. Chase rules were not located in the SRD sections read.
