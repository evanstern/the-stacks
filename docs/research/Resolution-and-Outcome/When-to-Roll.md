# When to Roll

_How sources say a DM decides between calling for a roll and simply deciding, who starts a roll, and how the player's stated intent and approach figure in. Neutral and descriptive._

## SRD 5.2.1 (CC-BY-4.0)

- The core loop has three steps: the GM describes a scene, the players describe what their characters do, and the GM narrates results. The GM asks for a roll only when something makes a task challenging, such as a lock or a hidden trap; otherwise the GM says the door opens. (SRD p.5, Rhythm of Play)
- A D20 Test is used "when the outcome of an action is uncertain" (SRD 5.2.1, CC-BY-4.0). Three kinds exist: ability check, saving throw, attack roll. (SRD p.6, D20 Tests)
- An ability check applies when a creature attempts something other than an attack that "has a chance of meaningful failure". The dice decide when the outcome is "uncertain and narratively interesting" (SRD 5.2.1, CC-BY-4.0). (SRD p.6, Ability Checks)
- The GM decides whether a D20 Test is warranted. (SRD p.180, Glossary: D20 Test)
- A saving throw is not normally chosen; the threat triggers it. The player may choose to fail a save without rolling. (SRD p.7, Saving Throws)
- An action outside the action list can be improvised: the player describes it, and the GM says whether it is possible and which D20 Test, if any, applies. (SRD p.10, Actions)
- Simple object interaction needs no roll: the player says the character moves a lever or opens a door and the GM describes the result. (SRD p.12, Interacting with Objects)
- The GM typically asks for a Wisdom (Perception) check only if the player describes searching near the hidden object. A search described far from the object fails regardless of the total. (SRD p.12, Finding Hidden Objects)
- Influence: the player describes how the character communicates. The GM then judges the monster Willing, Unwilling or Hesitant. Willing needs no check, Unwilling fails with no check, and only Hesitant gets a roll. (SRD p.184, Glossary: Influence action)
- Roleplaying can change an NPC's attitude on its own. A roll enters if the GM wants "an element of chance" (SRD 5.2.1, CC-BY-4.0); the GM then typically asks the player to take the Influence action. (SRD p.11, Social Interaction)
- Each character takes one action at a time; one action cannot combine Search and Influence. (SRD p.10, One Thing at a Time)
- Passive Perception lets the GM decide whether a creature notices something "without consciously making" a check (SRD 5.2.1, CC-BY-4.0), so a static score resolves noticing with no roll. (SRD p.186, Glossary: Passive Perception)

## Books

- DMG 2014 (Ch 8 Running the Game > Using Ability Scores, preamble): a two-question filter. Is the task so easy and free of conflict that failure is impossible? Is it so impossible that it cannot work? If both answers are no, some roll is appropriate. A roll is called only if failure carries a meaningful consequence.
- DMG 2014 (Ch 8 > Using Ability Scores > Ability Checks): a check tests whether a character succeeds at a task the character decided to attempt, so the player's declared intent starts it.
- DMG 2014 (Ch 8 > Ability Checks > Intelligence Check vs. Wisdom Check): a sidebar separates perception from inference. Wisdom checks cover what a character perceives; Intelligence checks cover why. A character with high Wisdom and low Intelligence can notice a clean patch of wall without deducing what it means.
- DMG 2014 (Ch 8 > The Role of Dice): three styles. Rolling with It uses dice for nearly everything and outcomes can surprise both sides. Ignoring the Dice uses dice mostly in combat and lets the DM decide non-combat outcomes from the quality of the plan. The Middle Path mixes both; the DM may declare automatic success or grant advantage at any time.
- DMG 2014 (Ch 8 > The Role of Dice > Ignoring the Dice): the example has players tapping walls or twisting a torch sconce and the DM revealing a secret door without a roll. The section names two costs: DM bias toward favored players or approaches, and fixation on one correct action.
- DMG 2014 (Ch 8 > The Role of Dice > Rolling with It): the named cost is that roleplaying can fade if players feel rolls, not decisions, always determine success.
- DMG 2014 (Ch 8 > Using Ability Scores > Attack Rolls): an attack roll is called when a character tries to hit a creature or object, particularly where armor, shield or cover could foil it.
- DMG 2014 (Ch 8 > Using Ability Scores > Saving Throws): a save is almost never by choice and is an instant reaction to something else's action. The DM calls for one when something bad happens to a character who has a chance to avoid it, and picks the ability when no rule names one.
- DMG 2014 (Ch 8 > Table Rules > Dice Rolling): the DM may roll for a player in secret when the player should not know the quality of the result (example: Insight against a possibly charmed baroness). The DM may also roll behind a screen for a Perception check when nothing is there, to preserve mystery, with a warning not to overuse it.
- XGtE (Ch 2 DM's Tools > Spellcasting > Identifying a Spell): identification is a player-initiated Intelligence (Arcana) check, made as a reaction while the spell is cast or as an action afterward. The character must have perceived the casting or its effect.
- TCoE (DM's Tools > Parleying with Monsters): parley can be improvised or run through the DMG social rules. Offering a monster something it wants earns advantage on communication checks; the offer is a player choice that changes the roll.

## Web

Angry GM

- Adjudicate Actions Like a Boss (https://theangrygm.com/adjudicate-actions-like-a-boss/): splits a declared action into an Intention (what the character wants, which defines success) and an Approach (how, which shapes what is possible and which mechanic applies). The article advises clarifying questions when either is unclear. It then asks whether the action is possible and whether it needs a roll. It rolls only when failure is possible and would change the character's situation; near-certain actions and free-retry actions with no cost succeed without dice.
- 5 Simple Rules for Dating My Teenaged Skill System (https://theangrygm.com/five-simple-rules-for-dating-my-teenaged-skill-system/): Rule 1, players describe an action or ask a question about the world and do not name a skill; the GM maps the action to a mechanic. Rule 2 gives three pre-roll questions: can it succeed, can it truly fail (failure needs a constraint such as a deadline, not just time), and does failure carry a cost. If any answer is no, the article recommends no roll.
- The Declare-Determine-Describe Cycle (https://theangrygm.com/declare-determine-describe/): the GM uses judgment first (can it succeed or fail, what success and failure mean, are there costs) and only then decides whether rules are needed to pick between outcomes. Rules are an optional tool used sparingly. The GM restates the declaration and waits for confirmation.
- The Tao of the Dice (https://theangrygm.com/tao-of-dice/): the GM, not the rulebook, decides when to roll, based on whether the outcome is genuinely uncertain. It advises against rolling for things already certain or not meant to be random, such as recognizing something outside a character's expertise. It describes a dial: roll more when players seem bored or overconfident, less when they seem frustrated.
- Help! My Players are Talking to Things! (https://theangrygm.com/help-my-players-are-talking-to-things/): applies the intention-plus-approach parse to conversation. Dice are rolled when the outcome is uncertain and the attempt has a meaningful cost or risk. Trivial requests with no plausible reason to refuse need no roll. An NPC needs an Objection (a reason not to help) for a social roll to exist.
- Dealing with Problematic Actions (https://theangrygm.com/problematic-actions/): automatic responses are not actions, because an action must be deliberate, interactive, external and visible. Knowledge the character already has is handed over by the GM without a player request. Finding hidden things requires the player to say how they physically search.
- Declaring Your Players' Social Actions (https://theangrygm.com/declaring-social-actions/): the GM translates casual speech and body language into a formal declaration (intended outcome plus approach), and works out what the action could realistically accomplish rather than relying only on the stated intent.

The Alexandrian

- The Art of Rulings (https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/4238/roleplaying-games/the-art-of-rulings): a skill check is called for only when the player declares an action specific enough to contain a meaningful choice, so the player's expertise activates the character's. The article warns against both a vague declaration that skips the choice and pixel-bitching, where every micro-detail must be narrated. Passive observation of obvious details needs no roll. Reactive checks cover less obvious things, and recognizing a familiar symbol should need neither a roll nor a player request.
- Failure for the Beginning GM (https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/46186/roleplaying-games/failure-for-the-beginning-gm): warns against unnecessary action checks; checks belong where failure would be interesting or consequential.

Sly Flourish

- Our Ability Check Toolbox (https://slyflourish.com/ability_check_toolbox.html): skip the roll when a task is nearly certain, nearly impossible or has no meaningful randomness, and narrate the outcome. Use passive scores for always-on skills (Perception, Insight, Investigation) and repetitive exploration checks, treating a static value as a floor. A well-chosen approach can lower the DC, grant advantage or skip the roll.
- Let Characters Automatically Succeed Sometimes (https://slyflourish.com/let_characters_auto_succeed.html): lists conditions for auto-success: the character plausibly could just do it, failure is not an option or would be boring, the task is not very difficult, and success showcases the character's proficiency. It ties this to features such as background, class, species, proficiencies and feats.
- Understanding 5e's Core Interaction (https://slyflourish.com/understanding_dnds_core_interaction.html): the loop is GM describes, players state what they want, GM determines the outcome. Routine or easy actions need no roll. When an action is risky or difficult, the GM sets the difficulty and ability and the player rolls a d20.

## Where sources disagree

- Passive scores, GM fiat or secret rolls for hidden things. Sly Flourish recommends passive scores as a floor for always-on skills and secret rolls sparingly (https://slyflourish.com/ability_check_toolbox.html). Angry GM prefers passive checks for knowledge (https://theangrygm.com/five-simple-rules-for-dating-my-teenaged-skill-system/). The Alexandrian argues a static passive value is effectively GM fiat that disguises the decision, and lists tradeoffs for each method (https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/40309/roleplaying-games/rulings-in-practice-perception-type-tests).
- Who rolls searches. Angry GM says players must state how they search (https://theangrygm.com/problematic-actions/). Sly Flourish says players usually roll their own checks and secret GM rolls are for sparing use (https://slyflourish.com/ability_check_toolbox.html). The Tao of the Dice allows secret GM rolls for information-sensitive checks (https://theangrygm.com/tao-of-dice/).
- Rolling for social action versus player-driven resolution. Angry GM recommends leaning toward a die roll for social actions (https://theangrygm.com/resolving-social-actions/) and says player eloquence should not change the odds (https://theangrygm.com/problematic-actions/). Sly Flourish says a charismatic player's approach can be the baseline without a roll (https://slyflourish.com/running_intrigue_adventures.html). The Alexandrian argues that a single check removes the player from the key step of a riddle (https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/48452/roleplaying-games/random-gm-tip-rolling-for-riddles).
- Book versus web: the DMG 2014 filter for rolling (trivial or impossible means no roll; consequence for failure needed) and Angry GM's pre-roll questions (can it succeed, can it truly fail, is there a cost) ask overlapping questions; the web article adds that failure must be constrained by something other than time.
- Book versus web: DMG 2014 describes the three dice styles as options with named costs. The web articles in this set each describe one method (judgment first; dial by table mood; auto-success) and do not present the three-style frame.
- Edition divergence: Search, Study and Influence are named actions in SRD 5.2.1 (p.10, glossary p.187, p.189, p.184). DMG 2014 treats the same activity as ability checks the DM calls and keeps the Intelligence-versus-Wisdom split as a sidebar; SRD 5.2.1 builds the split into Search (Wisdom) and Study (Intelligence).
- Edition divergence: DMG 2014 social resolution is a four-step procedure ending in a Charisma check; SRD 5.2.1 compresses it into the Influence action, where only a Hesitant monster rolls.
- Edition divergence: passive scores. SRD p.186 gives a formula with +5 and -5 for Advantage and Disadvantage; DMG 2014 refers to passive Wisdom (Perception) in examples without restating a formula. The comparison with the 2014 Player's Handbook was not made.
