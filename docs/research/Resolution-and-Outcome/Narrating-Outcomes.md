# Narrating Outcomes

_How sources divide the telling of results between DM and players: who announces success or failure, how hits, misses and partial results are put into words, and what the DM reveals. Neutral and descriptive._

## SRD 5.2.1 (CC-BY-4.0)

- The division of labour is fixed: the GM describes the scene, the players describe what their characters do, and the GM narrates results. Describing results "often leads to another decision point" (SRD 5.2.1, CC-BY-4.0). (SRD p.5, Rhythm of Play)
- Outside combat the GM ensures every character has a chance to act and decides how to resolve their activity. (SRD p.5, Rhythm of Play, step 2)
- For simple interactions the player states the action and the GM describes what happens. (SRD p.12, Interacting with Objects)
- The GM "tells players whether their rolls are successful" (SRD 5.2.1, CC-BY-4.0), so the GM owns the success or failure announcement. (SRD p.6, D20 Tests)
- The player decides how the character thinks, acts and talks, and may use an active or a descriptive approach. The GM uses the NPC's personality and the character's actions to decide the NPC's reaction. (SRD p.10, Roleplaying)
- Example NPC reactions: a cowardly bandit buckles at threats; a stubborn merchant refuses if badgered; a vain dragon laps up flattery. (SRD p.11, Roleplaying examples)
- Roleplaying can change attitude without a roll. If an NPC's reaction depends partly on chance, the roll supplements the player's own words. (SRD p.11, Social Interaction)
- Bloodied (half HP or fewer) has no game effect on its own but gives a labeled fiction state for describing wounds. (SRD p.17, Bloodied)
- Hidden creatures that attack reveal their location when the attack hits or misses. (SRD p.183, Hide; p.14)
- An attacker guessing at an unseen target's location has Disadvantage, and if the target is not there the attack misses. (SRD p.14, Unseen Attackers and Targets)
- The GM chooses between average and rolled monster damage. (SRD p.189, Damage Notation)

## Books

- DMG 2014 (Ch 8 > Combat > Tracking Monster Hit Points): players ask how hurt a monster looks. The DM need not reveal hit points; below half, it is fair to say the monster shows visible wounds. Calling a monster bloodied at half gives players a sense of progress and a cue for using strong abilities. The section suggests giving each monster in a group a distinctive feature for narration.
- DMG 2014 (Ch 8 > Social Interaction > Roleplaying > Being the NPC): show rather than tell; have an NPC behave as a shallow person would instead of announcing it. Responses should add twists, and NPCs should reflect ideals, bonds and flaws.
- DMG 2014 (Ch 8 > Roleplaying > Using Your Voice / Using Your Face and Arms): vary voice for effect, give distinct voices to NPCs speaking together, keep the adventurers as the focus rather than NPCs talking to each other, and use facial expressions and gestures to carry emotion.
- DMG 2014 (Ch 8 > Roleplaying > Engaging the Players): draw quieter players in by creating NPC contacts for specific characters or by asking a player what the character is doing while others talk.
- DMG 2014 (Ch 8 > Resolution and Consequences > Success at a Cost): narration shapes for partial success: the hit lands but the sword is knocked away; the fireball is mostly dodged but the character ends up prone; the kobold talks but screams.
- DMG 2014 (Ch 8 > Critical Success or Failure): narration may reflect an extreme roll (broken thieves' tools; an extra clue), though the rules do not require it.
- DMG 2014 (Ch 8 > Ability Checks > Multiple Ability Checks): a failed lie is narrated as the guard no longer believing the same story; the guard's reaction removes that approach.
- DMG 2014 (Ch 8 > Combat > Improvising Damage): narrated hazards tied to dice figures (burning coals or a poison needle 1d10, lightning 2d10, rubble 4d10, lava stream 10d10).
- DMG 2014 (Ch 8 > Table Rules > Dice Rolling): where rolls are visible, the DM's narration of outcomes reads as impartial; behind a screen it can hide enemy strength, preserve mystery or smooth a spike. The text lists costs of both.
- DMG 2014 (Ch 8 > Exploration > Special Travel Pace): travel can be summarized rather than narrated step by step.
- VRGtR (Horror Adventures > Running Horror Games > Describing Horror): describe a threat indirectly and in stages as it draws closer, shifting senses at each stage. Let Players Fill Gaps advises leaving details blank so the players' imagination does the work.
- VRGtR (Horror Adventures > Horror Pacing > Idle Uneasiness / Out of Time): slow, sensory narration during build-up, with questions to players about what their characters are thinking or fearing. In action beats narrate in short quick bursts, favor impact over senses, and push the players to react.
- VRGtR (Horror Adventures > Horror Pacing > Parallel Scenes): cut between split-party scenes in short beats and cut away at the exciting moment so players wait for resolution.
- XGtE (Ch 2 > Spellcasting > Perceiving a Caster at Work): a spell is perceptible to others only if it has a verbal, somatic or material component or a visible effect. Subtle casting makes the cast invisible; an unseen charm gives the target no sign. What the DM narrates therefore depends on the components used.
- XGtE (Ch 2 > Spellcasting > Invalid Spell Targets): when the target is invalid, the DM narrates a save success without revealing why.
- TCoE (DM's Tools > Environmental Hazards > Magical Phenomena): environmental events are given as narrated phenomena (a flaywind that can be recognized with a DC 15 check one minute before it strikes).

## Web

Angry GM

- The Declare-Determine-Describe Cycle (https://theangrygm.com/declare-determine-describe/): three steps. The player declares (complete, concise, in world terms), the GM determines, and the GM describes. It criticizes bare results with no narration and recommends restating the action as accomplished or failed.
- Adjudicate Actions Like a Boss (https://theangrygm.com/adjudicate-actions-like-a-boss/): narrate the outcome in about one sentence: restate the player's action, then join the outcome with and or but. Mention consequences when natural. Echoing the player's words makes results feel responsive.
- How to Talk to Players: The Art of Narration (https://theangrygm.com/how-to-talk-to-players-the-art-of-narration/): narration is clear, concise communication rather than vivid prose. It lists outcome description, scene setting (about five short sentences ending on the most pressing threat), transitions and exposition. Scene setting aims to invite player action.
- Dealing with Problematic Actions (https://theangrygm.com/problematic-actions/): the GM speaks only facts: what characters perceive, hear and know. The GM describes signs and cues (a boot visible beneath a tapestry, an NPC's tone and body language) and lets players interpret them.
- Help! My Players are Talking to Things! (https://theangrygm.com/help-my-players-are-talking-to-things/): an NPC reply built as response, reason, opening. The reply signals success or failure, explains why talk continues, and offers a way forward. A deflection is an opening that goes nowhere. If the scene ends, the GM states so clearly.
- Resolving Social Actions (https://theangrygm.com/resolving-social-actions/): the GM acts out the result as a believable person would respond rather than as a mechanical readout. If an outcome looks illogical, the GM can invent a plausible reason and make it established.
- Inviting the Principal PC to Act (https://theangrygm.com/inviting-pcs-to-act/): speak to the player and about the character in the same statement (name both when inviting action), and alternate character names with short in-world descriptors.
- The Tao of the Dice (https://theangrygm.com/tao-of-dice/) and Declaring Your Players' Social Actions (https://theangrygm.com/declaring-social-actions/): the GM lets a rolled result stand and does not fudge.

Sly Flourish

- Empowering Player-Driven Storytelling (https://slyflourish.com/empowering_player_storytelling.html): hand description to players: ask what happened to the character after a notable roll, ask new players to describe a killing blow, and incorporate off-hand details players add.
- Roll to Target (https://slyflourish.com/roll_to_target.html): when the GM deliberately picks a target, the GM states the monster's reasoning aloud. The article gives no standard format for narrating a random result.

The Alexandrian

- Failure for the Beginning GM (https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/46186/roleplaying-games/failure-for-the-beginning-gm): a failed check that still succeeds in the fiction carries a complication from a default list (extra cost, damage, lost or broken equipment, extra time, being discovered); results should stand.

## Where sources disagree

- Whether dice results stand. Angry GM and the Alexandrian say results should stand (https://theangrygm.com/tao-of-dice/, https://theangrygm.com/declaring-social-actions/, https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/46186/roleplaying-games/failure-for-the-beginning-gm). DMG 2014 (Table Rules > Dice Rolling) describes the DM softening a lethal second crit behind a screen, with the caveat not to overuse it and not to let on. Sly Flourish has an article on when and how to cheat that this pass did not fetch, so no disagreement is logged from it.
- Narrating partial progress. Angry GM treats partial progress as an outcome to use sparingly (https://theangrygm.com/adjudicate-actions-like-a-boss/). Sly Flourish and the Alexandrian treat graded or complicated results as a routine tool (https://slyflourish.com/group_skill_checks.html, https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/46186/roleplaying-games/failure-for-the-beginning-gm).
- Book versus web on who describes: SRD 5.2.1 and DMG 2014 keep description of results with the GM; Sly Flourish describes handing description to players after a roll (https://slyflourish.com/empowering_player_storytelling.html). DMG 2014 Plot Points also lets a player add a fact the table accepts.
- Book versus web on form: DMG 2014 advice on narration covers voice, face and arms, and showing NPC traits through behavior; Angry GM frames narration as clear and concise communication and gives one-sentence and five-sentence formats.
- Hidden versus open rolls. DMG 2014 lists costs of both visible and screened rolls. The Alexandrian argues hiding a roll's purpose preserves character perspective (https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/39142/roleplaying-games/art-of-rulings-12-hidden-vs-open-difficulty-numbers).
- Edition divergence: Bloodied. SRD p.17 defines it with no game effect; DMG 2014 treats it as a descriptive cue.
