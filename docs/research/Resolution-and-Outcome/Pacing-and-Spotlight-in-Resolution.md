# Pacing and Spotlight in Resolution

_How sources connect the amount of dice rolling and the shape of a resolution to table pace and to who gets the spotlight. Neutral and descriptive._

## SRD 5.2.1 (CC-BY-4.0)

- Resolution is a short loop: describe, declare, narrate. Outside combat the GM "ensures that every character has a chance to act" (SRD 5.2.1, CC-BY-4.0). (SRD p.5, Rhythm of Play)
- One action at a time limits how many things resolve per turn. (SRD p.10, One Thing at a Time)
- A character may skip movement or action; Dodge or Ready is suggested for an undecided player. (SRD p.14, Doing Nothing on Your Turn)
- Travel can be summarized without calculating distances; pace rules apply only if the GM wants them. Combat and movement use exact distances only when every second matters. (SRD p.13, Travel)
- One Initiative roll serves a group of identical monsters. (SRD p.13, Initiative)
- Optional: use Initiative scores (10 + Dexterity modifier, +5 or -5 for Advantage or Disadvantage) instead of rolling. (SRD p.184, Initiative)
- The GM can choose a stat block's average damage instead of rolling. (SRD p.189, Damage Notation)
- The group is told to lead with whoever has the right skill, which routes the spotlight by proficiency. (SRD p.11, Social Interaction)

## Books

- DMG 2014 (Ch 8 > Table Rules > Rolling Attacks and Damage): rolling attack and damage dice together speeds up the table.
- DMG 2014 (Ch 8 > Combat > Handling Mobs): replaces a large number of attack rolls with the mob-attack lookup, ignoring crits and returning to individual rolls as numbers dwindle.
- DMG 2014 (Ch 8 > Combat > Adjudicating Areas of Effect): without a grid, go with your gut or use a quick targets-per-area formula (cone: size / 10 rounded up; cube: size / 5; sphere: radius / 5; line: length / 30) with a 1d3 adjustment. The text states it aims for simplicity over spatial precision.
- DMG 2014 (Ch 8 > Combat > Tracking Initiative): hidden, visible and index-card methods; calling out who is next prompts the next player to plan. A player can track initiative to lighten DM load.
- DMG 2014 (Ch 8 > Combat > Adjudicating Reaction Timing): follow the reaction's stated timing; if none is stated, the reaction occurs after its trigger finishes.
- DMG 2014 (Ch 8 > Combat > Combining Game Effects): only the most potent of same-named effects applies, which prevents stacking disputes.
- DMG 2014 (Ch 8 > Ability Checks > Multiple Ability Checks): the ten-times-time rule converts a retry loop into automatic success so play does not stall on repeated rolls.
- DMG 2014 (Ch 8 > Using Ability Scores > Difficulty Class > Variant: Automatic Success): reduces rolls on easy tasks, with the cost of predictability.
- DMG 2014 (Ch 8 > Chases): adds random elements so faster creatures do not trivially catch slower ones; each participant takes one action and a move; free Dashes are limited; one Stealth-versus-passive check per round decides escape. Complication tables (urban and wilderness) add events.
- DMG 2014 (Ch 8 > Exploration > Map Travel Pace / Special Travel Pace): travel resolution uses a pace and distance lookup so a journey takes few decisions.
- DMG 2014 (Ch 8 > Social Interaction > Engaging the Players): give each player something to do during roleplay-heavy sessions by matching activities to player preferences, creating NPC contacts for specific characters, and drawing in quieter players, in or out of character; link long conversations to combat connections for action-focused players.
- DMG 2014 (Ch 8 > Inspiration > Awarding Inspiration): target about one inspiration per character per session, distributed across play styles (roleplaying, heroism, victory, genre emulation) without favoring one style. Spreading the spotlight evenly is part of the stated fairness aim.
- DMG 2014 (Ch 8 > Table Rules > Small Groups): players may control more than one character; do not force reluctant players or show favoritism.
- VRGtR (Horror Adventures > Horror Pacing): build tension and release it, mix social, exploration and combat scenes, keep each scene relevant to the story, and plan for less session time than the full session length.
- VRGtR (Horror Adventures > Horror Pacing > Parallel Scenes): keep split-party scenes short and cut away on the exciting event.
- VRGtR (Horror Adventures > Running Horror Games > Engaging Players): if players seem disengaged, check in; offer two or three written options privately; call a break if issues persist.
- VRGtR (Horror Adventures > Horror Pacing > End on Cliffhangers): end the session on a new threat or change and call a break before players react.

## Web

- Pacing for the Beginning GM (https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/49547/roleplaying-games/pacing-for-the-beginning-gm): each scene has an agenda (the question it answers); move on if the answer is obvious. In logistical scenes the final successful roll is a natural cut point, roughly a minute after the goal. In roleplaying scenes, cut at the second lull. In combat, speed matters: batch dice, roll NPC dice while players resolve theirs, and keep initiative clear.
- Inviting the Principal PC to Act (https://theangrygm.com/inviting-pcs-to-act/): names one Principal PC per scene (the best-placed character, or the one who succeeded on a relevant check) and sends invitations to act only to that player. If the player stalls, the GM can hold them and move on; silence is the main threat to pacing.
- 5 Simple Rules for Dating My Teenaged Skill System (https://theangrygm.com/five-simple-rules-for-dating-my-teenaged-skill-system/): unnecessary rolls waste table time, so rolls are saved for consequential actions. Repeated rolls are tied to a visible countdown and used sparingly.
- GM Don't List #2: Rolling to Failure (https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/38798/roleplaying-games/gm-dont-list-2-rolling-to-failure): a 16-check corridor search shows how per-unit checks slow play; stretching resolution is a deliberate pacing tool only in key tense moments.
- Handling Group Skill Checks (https://slyflourish.com/group_skill_checks.html): a full group check is a spotlight tool; rolling individually shows who succeeds and breaks up narration; if one success suffices the roll can be skipped.
- Let Characters Automatically Succeed Sometimes (https://slyflourish.com/let_characters_auto_succeed.html): auto-success keeps the game moving and spotlights what each character is good at; the article uses rolling for every item of clothing as an example of trivial checks.
- Rolling Lots of D20s? Assume One Quarter Succeeds (https://slyflourish.com/one_quarter_succeed.html): the one-quarter rule cuts dice time and returns quickly to narration.
- The Tao of the Dice (https://theangrygm.com/tao-of-dice/): each roll interrupts play, especially in social scenes; the article also warns against long stretches with no rolls.
- Random GM Tip: Dogpiling the Check (https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/49184/roleplaying-games/random-gm-tip-dogpiling-the-check): everyone rolls at once, or reactive checks are anticipated, to avoid slow sequential rolling when the result does not matter.

## Where sources disagree

- Rolls as interruption versus rolls as spotlight. Angry GM's Tao of the Dice notes each roll interrupts play and also warns against long stretches without rolls (https://theangrygm.com/tao-of-dice/). Sly Flourish treats a full group check as a way to show individuals succeeding (https://slyflourish.com/group_skill_checks.html) and auto-success as a way to spotlight proficiency (https://slyflourish.com/let_characters_auto_succeed.html).
- Stretching resolution. The Alexandrian treats stretched resolution as a deliberate tool only in key tense moments (https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/38798/roleplaying-games/gm-dont-list-2-rolling-to-failure). VRGtR Horror Pacing builds slow, sensory narration during build-up and short bursts in action beats.
- Speed tools. DMG 2014 offers lookups (mob attacks, pace tables, ten-times-time success); Sly Flourish offers the one-quarter assumption for many simultaneous rolls. Both reduce rolls; they differ in what they replace (individual attack rolls and retries, versus a batch of simultaneous d20s).
- Spotlight by proficiency or by rotation. SRD p.11 routes social checks to the character with the right skill; Angry GM names one Principal PC per scene, the best-placed character or one who succeeded on a relevant check; DMG 2014 Engaging the Players gives each player something to do through NPC contacts and activity matching.
- Edition divergence: Initiative scores. SRD p.184 offers an optional score in place of rolling; the DMG 2014 sections read offer initiative-tracking methods without a fixed-score variant.
