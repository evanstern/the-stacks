# Table Talk and Questions to the DM

_How sources treat out-of-character talk, clarifying questions between player and DM, what characters automatically know, and metagaming. Neutral and descriptive._

## SRD 5.2.1 (CC-BY-4.0)

- A player must state where the character searches; the GM's answer follows from that stated position. (SRD p.12, Finding Hidden Objects)
- The standard channel for a player's can-I-try question is the improvised action: the player describes it and the GM says whether it can be done and what roll applies. (SRD p.10, Actions)
- The player may choose to fail a save without rolling; the choice is communicated, not hidden. (SRD p.7, Saving Throws)
- The GM is told to discuss any fear or mental stress rules with the players at the start of the campaign. (SRD p.196, Fear and Mental Stress)
- A character can speak in brief utterances and gestures during a turn at no cost; extended explanation or persuasion takes an action. (SRD p.14, Communicating)
- The GM uses passive scores to decide whether a creature notices something without a player announcing a check, which covers what a character automatically knows. (SRD p.186, Passive Perception)

## Books

- DMG 2014 (Ch 8 > Table Rules > Table Talk): expectations on three points. Make clear who is speaking, the character or the player. Decide how to handle a player sharing information the character does not have or could not share (unconscious, dead, far away). Decide whether players may retract what they just said their characters did.
- DMG 2014 (Ch 8 > Table Rules > Metagame Thinking): metagame thinking treats the game as a game, such as a player saying the DM would not send a monster this strong. The remedy is a gentle prompt asking what the characters think. The section also suggests setting up situations where negotiation or retreat is the sensible answer, which makes strength-matching less automatic.
- DMG 2014 (Ch 8 > Table Rules > Rules Discussions): choose a table policy: pause for a debate or let the DM rule and continue. If a rule is glossed over, note it (a player can do this) and revisit later.
- DMG 2014 (Ch 8 > Table Rules > Dice Rolling): roll in view by default. Decide rules for dropped or cocked dice. The DM decides between rolling openly (shows impartiality) and behind a screen (keeps strength unknown, allows fudging, preserves mystery). Rolling secretly for a player stops the player from knowing how good the total is.
- DMG 2014 (Ch 8 > Ability Checks > Intelligence Check vs. Wisdom Check): the split between what a character notices and what a character concludes is a guide for what the DM reveals from a roll.
- DMG 2014 (Ch 8 > Social Interaction > 2. Conversation > Determining Characteristics): the party does not start knowing an NPC's ideal, bond or flaw. A guess risks shifting attitude the wrong way. An Insight check after enough conversation can reveal one trait; a failure by 10 or more gives false information.
- DMG 2014 (Ch 8 > Social Interaction > 2. Conversation > Changing Attitude): the DM decides whether a player's framing touches what matters to the NPC. An attitude generally shifts by at most one step in a single interaction.
- DMG 2014 (Ch 8 > Exploration > Noticing Other Creatures): automatic noticing occurs when neither side is stealthy and the parties are within sight or hearing; otherwise Stealth against passive Perception decides it.
- DMG 2014 (Ch 8 > Inspiration > When Do You Award Inspiration?): awarding before an action tells players what earns it and can feel like steering their choices; awarding after preserves flow and agency. The text recommends starting with awarding after.
- DMG 2014 (Ch 8 > Inspiration > Ignoring Inspiration): some DMs think inspiration adds metagame thinking and skip it.
- DMG 2014 (Ch 8 > Table Rules > Missing Players): four options for absent players' characters: another player runs it, the DM runs it, the character is written out, or the character fades into the background with no in-world explanation.
- DMG 2014 (Ch 9 > Adventuring Options > Plot Points): introducing a player-authored fact is a metagame-to-fiction channel by design: the player proposes, the table discusses, and the fact becomes true.
- TCoE (DM's Tools > Session Zero > Social Contract): standard commitments. The DM runs a fun, fair game, lets each player contribute, gives each character moments to shine, and listens. Players let the DM direct the campaign and settle rules, and respect and support each other.
- TCoE (DM's Tools > Session Zero > Hard and Soft Limits): limits can be shared privately and presented without attribution. In-game and out-of-game limits are both named, with the instruction to check the list again over the campaign.
- TCoE (DM's Tools > Session Zero > Game Customization / House Rules): ask players which pillars they favor and how much humor they want. Present house rules as experiments.
- VRGtR (Horror Adventures > Preparing for Horror and Running Horror Games): a Horror Content Survey, session zero boundaries, check-ins after breaks (what characters hope or worry might happen), an X-Card, and a comfort check when the game gets intense.
- XGtE (Ch 2 > Spellcasting > Perceiving a Caster at Work): by default characters do not know a spell was cast unless it has a noticeable component or effect.

## Web

Angry GM

- Adjudicate Actions Like a Boss (https://theangrygm.com/adjudicate-actions-like-a-boss/): the GM asks clarifying questions when intention or approach is unclear, and warns players when failure is obvious so they do not lose anything through a misunderstanding.
- The Declare-Determine-Describe Cycle (https://theangrygm.com/declare-determine-describe/): the GM restates the declaration and waits for confirmation, checks for missing details, and trains players toward complete, concise, in-world declarations. Players may speak about their characters in third person and need not perform fully in character.
- Help! My Players are Talking to Things! (https://theangrygm.com/help-my-players-are-talking-to-things/): first-person speech and third-person description are both valid and neither is more immersive. If the approach is unclear, the GM may ask the player or have the NPC ask in character. The GM should not withhold what an NPC wants, since players cannot read minds.
- Inviting the Principal PC to Act (https://theangrygm.com/inviting-pcs-to-act/): when a player asks what their character knows, the GM answers and then turns to the Principal Character. Open table questions are treated as actions, resolved briefly, then handed back.
- The Tao of the Dice (https://theangrygm.com/tao-of-dice/): a die result can reveal hidden information (a high search roll implies nothing was missed); the article advises secret rolls for information-sensitive checks.
- Dealing with Problematic Actions (https://theangrygm.com/problematic-actions/): the article does not directly address clarifying questions; its position is that the GM hands over automatic knowledge unasked.

The Alexandrian

- Rulings in Practice: Perception-Type Tests (https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/40309/roleplaying-games/rulings-in-practice-perception-type-tests): a passive check leaks metagame knowledge because rolling signals something may be hidden. It compares four methods: player rolls; GM secret rolls (hides the check but needs score tracking); static passive values (fast, but argued to be GM fiat in disguise); and a Traveller: 2300-derived uncertain-task method with paired rolls and no, some or total truth results.
- Random GM Tip: Dogpiling the Check (https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/49184/roleplaying-games/random-gm-tip-dogpiling-the-check): retry by a second character after a known failure is metagaming; secret checks reduce it, though uncertainty can still encourage dogpiling.
- Art of Rulings 12: Hidden vs. Open Difficulty Numbers (https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/39142/roleplaying-games/art-of-rulings-12-hidden-vs-open-difficulty-numbers): open numbers reduce miscommunication and are-you-sure problems because players perceive the world through imprecise verbal description. Hiding the number or purpose of a roll preserves a character-level perspective.
- Ptolus: Firewalling Player Knowledge (https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/43352/roleplaying-games/ptolus-running-the-campaign-firewalling-player-knowledge): keep player knowledge separate from character knowledge: state which characters perceive each event, give brief recaps when cutting between split groups, and use private conversations or notes at some pacing cost. Withholding only works if information is truly hidden; asking players to pretend not to know is not enough.

Sly Flourish

- Our Ability Check Toolbox (https://slyflourish.com/ability_check_toolbox.html): the GM rolls privately when the character may not know whether they succeeded (traps, secret doors, poison), sparingly since players usually roll their own checks.

## Where sources disagree

- Passive scores, GM fiat or secret rolls for hidden things: see [[When-to-Roll]]. Sly Flourish recommends passive scores and sparing secret rolls; Angry GM prefers passive checks for knowledge; the Alexandrian argues static passive values are GM fiat in disguise and says a passive check leaks metagame knowledge.
- Who rolls searches. Angry GM says players do not roll their own perception checks and must state how they search (https://theangrygm.com/problematic-actions/). Sly Flourish says players usually roll their own checks (https://slyflourish.com/ability_check_toolbox.html). The Tao of the Dice allows secret GM rolls for information-sensitive checks.
- Hidden versus open DCs and purposes. The Alexandrian hides numbers characters could not observe and reveals those they could. Angry GM's Declare-Determine-Describe advises warning players of foreseeable risks and costs before they commit. The Alexandrian's comments record the author defending hiding a roll's purpose to preserve character perspective, against a commenter who wanted purposes stated.
- Book versus web on hiding rolls: DMG 2014 lets the DM roll secretly for a player when the player should not know the quality of the result and lists costs of both visible and screened rolls. The web set argues both ways, with the Alexandrian's hidden-by-default posture and Angry GM's warn-before-commit posture.
- Book versus web on metagame prompts: DMG 2014 suggests a gentle prompt to ask what the characters think. The Alexandrian suggests physical separation of knowledge (private conversations, notes) and says asking players to pretend not to know is not enough.
- Edition divergence: passive scores and Heroic Inspiration differ between SRD 5.2.1 and DMG 2014 as listed in [[Setting-DCs-and-Modifiers]].
